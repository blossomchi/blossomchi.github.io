Search-and-rescue planner for microcontroller-class robots

Accepted for oral presentation: CSAI Days 2026, 29 October 2026

A low-cost ground robot decides where to search next and which of its cheap, unreliable sensors is worth spending battery on.

Scope. The results below come from a stochastic simulation of the decision logic (23,400 episodes, with released code), using documented synthetic sensor models. The ESP32 platform is planned but has not been assembled, so no hardware timing, energy or detection results are claimed, and the claim that the method fits microcontroller-class resources is a design claim, not a measurement. Expect the numbers to shift once real sensor noise replaces the modelling assumptions.

The question

Most search-and-rescue robots assume LiDAR mapping, depth cameras or a team of robots on a reliable link. A robot built from a handful of cheap sensors has none of that: no dense map, little computing power, a small battery, and sensors that are individually unreliable. A passive infrared sensor fires on a curtain moving in a draught, and a temperature reading cannot tell a person from a warm pipe. The question is not whether such a robot can explore, but how it should prioritise where to look, which sensor to spend energy on and when to trust a detection while watching hazard, navigation risk and remaining charge.

The method

Detection is two-stage: screen, then verify. Victim likelihood for each candidate region is an equal-weight mix of a passive-infrared cue and a temperature cue (0.50 each, fitted on separate calibration episodes). A region is proposed for close-range verification only when that likelihood reaches a threshold. Verification moves closer and re-samples, and a candidate is logged only if the average still clears the threshold and a hazard cross-check passes. The cross-check withholds a victim call when the gas reading signals a hazard, unless both motion and temperature corroborate.

The robot picks its next region with a Search Priority Score:

Pi(t) = α(Bt)·Vi − β·Hi − γ·Ri − δ(Bt)·Di

Here V is victim likelihood, H hazard from gas readings, R navigation risk from ultrasonic range variance and tilt, D travel distance and B the battery remaining. The two battery-dependent weights are smooth logistic curves that shift emphasis toward confirmed leads and short trips as charge falls. A separate reserve rule stops the search and returns to base when the battery reaches a set floor.

When the evidence for a region is ambiguous, the robot does not sample every sensor. It picks the single action, from re-reading temperature, re-reading motion, moving 0.5 closer, or abandoning the region, with the best information-to-cost ratio:

A(a) = IG(a)·Rel(a) / [ Ecost(a) + ε + λ·Risk(a) ]
  1. SenseRead the cheap, always-on sensors: motion, ultrasonic, tilt.
  2. Score regionsCompute the Search Priority Score for every candidate region.
  3. TravelHead for the highest-scoring region and re-plan around obstacles.
  4. ScreenUse the cheap motion cue as a gate before spending energy on temperature or gas.
  5. Resolve doubtIf the evidence is ambiguous, take the one cheapest useful sensing action, repeatedly, within a budget of four.
  6. Verify or rejectLog a verified candidate location for human checking, or drop the lead.
  7. Re-planUpdate the battery estimate and return to base when the reserve is reached.

Planned hardware

An ESP32 DevKit with a sensor shield, one HC-SR501 motion sensor, three HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors, an MLX90614 infrared thermometer, an MQ-2 gas sensor, an MPU6050 inertial unit and a battery-voltage module, driving four DC motors through an L298N on a four-wheel chassis. There is no camera and no GPU, so the whole bill of materials stays within reach of a student or volunteer team. A priced bill of materials will be published before any cost claim is made.

What worked

  • Active sensing roughly halved sensor activations (37.1 to 18.2 per episode) and cut energy per verified detection by 24%, from 768 to 585 units. The saving appeared in every scenario, from 12% to 40%, and it stayed positive (6.5% to 32%) across a 27-setting sweep of the assumed sensor parameters.
  • It did this with no detectable change in detection rate (+0.017, 95% interval −0.017 to +0.049) or false-positive rate.
  • A variant that always takes one temperature sample raised the detection rate by 7.6 percentage points over the active policy for 18.7 extra energy units per episode, and had the lowest energy per verified detection of all policies (546). It is the version recommended for hardware trials.
  • The hazard cross-check mattered: removing it doubled the false-positive rate (0.016 to 0.036), because hot hazard regions otherwise look like victims once temperature is sampled.
  • The reserve rule prevented stranding. Without it, the proposed method failed in every battery-capped episode.

What did not

  • The motion gate costs recall. It passes only about 70% of true victims, so roughly 30% are rejected before temperature is ever sampled. That matters because unconscious or pinned victims are the least likely to trigger a motion sensor.
  • The battery-dependent weights showed no benefit. Without them, mission success under the 30% cap was 53.3%, no worse than the full method's 50.0%. Baselines given the same reserve rule also reached 38% to 45%, and the proposed method's edge over the best of them was not significant after correction. Most of the survival advantage came from having a return rule at all.
  • The hazard term in the score did not reduce hazard exposure. With 12 regions and a 600-unit battery the robot visits almost every region, so visit order matters little.
  • Absolute detection rates are low (0.38 to 0.49) because the thresholds are conservative and were not tuned, so the comparisons are best read in relative terms. Fusing motion and temperature improved the classification F1 score (0.632 against 0.582 for temperature alone) but barely changed ranking quality.

Code, parameters and per-episode results: github.com/blossomchi/Search-and-Rescue-Robots.

Next steps

Assemble the platform and validate on hardware, starting with recorded sensor traces replayed in a standard simulator such as Gazebo or Webots. Then tune the detection thresholds on operating curves, try likelihood-ratio fusion, compare against a stronger POMDP or tree-search planner, add a camera cue, learn the weights online, and test with a search-and-rescue partner. A detection is meant as a lead for human verification, and both missed victims and false alarms carry real costs. A related design entry, Risk-Aware Human-Robot Coordination for Emergency Search and Rescue, is in preparation for the HRI 2027 Student Design Competition.
